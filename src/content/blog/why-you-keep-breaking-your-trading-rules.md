Almost every trader has at least one rule they know is correct and still break on a regular basis. It is rarely a knowledge problem, most traders can explain exactly why the rule exists. The gap is between knowing a rule and following it under the specific conditions where breaking it feels justified.

## The rule was written in a different state than it gets tested in

A rule like "never risk more than one percent per trade" is written calmly, outside of any live position. It gets tested in a completely different state: after two losses in a row, with the account down for the day, and a setup in front of you that feels like the one that will turn things around. The version of you that wrote the rule and the version of you that is deciding whether to follow it are, in a meaningful sense, operating with different priorities.

## Rule breaking is not random

If you look back at your own history, rule breaks tend to cluster around a small number of situations rather than happening evenly across all your trades. The two most common triggers are a recent loss, which creates pressure to win it back, and a recent winning streak, which creates a false sense that the usual caution is no longer necessary.

Both situations share something: they are moments when the last outcome, not the current setup, is driving the decision.

## What actually helps

General resolve, deciding to simply try harder to follow the rules, tends not to hold up under the exact pressure it needs to withstand. What works better is narrowing the problem: identifying the one or two rules that get broken most often, and the specific situation that precedes it, then building a concrete mechanical barrier for that exact moment. A fixed cooldown after a loss, or a hard cap on position size that cannot be overridden in the moment, removes the decision rather than relying on willpower to make a different one.

Tracking when and how often a specific rule actually gets broken is also more useful than a vague sense of "I need to be more disciplined." The data usually shows a narrower, more fixable problem than it feels like from the inside.
